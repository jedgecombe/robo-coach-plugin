# Marathon fuelling and nutrition: the evidence

Shareable note — doses are per kilogram, no athlete data. The private working version, with
absolute quantities and this block's schedule, lives outside `notes/shared/`.

Written because a 14-week plan can be structurally excellent and say **nothing at all** about
fuelling, which is how ours started. For a marathon attempt near the edge of current fitness,
race fuelling is the largest uncontrolled variable — and unlike training structure, fixing it
costs nothing in load.

## 1. Carbohydrate during the race

| | Target |
|---|---|
| Rate | **60–90 g/h**, at roughly 2:1 glucose:fructose |
| Total, 3-hour marathon | **180–270 g** — 5–7 gels, or gels plus carb drink |
| Upper end | 100–120 g/h, used routinely in elite competition |

The 2:1 ratio matters: fructose recruits GLUT5 alongside SGLT1, lifting the absorption ceiling
above the ~60 g/h single-transporter limit. In trained runners,
[7 of 9 tolerated 120 g/h](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7284742/) with lower
internal load and lower 24-hour muscle-damage markers than at 60 or 90 g/h.

**Absorption is trainable, and that's the part plans miss.** A
[2023 systematic review](https://runnersconnect.net/marathon-gut-training/) found two weeks of
gut training at 30–90 g/h cut GI discomfort by up to **47%** and carbohydrate malabsorption by
**45–54%**, with time-trial improvements following. Intestinal transporter expression responds
to habitual exposure.

So prescribe the carbohydrate rate on long runs the way you prescribe pace, and escalate it:

| Phase | Rate | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Early base | 60 g/h | Establish the habit; find products that sit well |
| Mid block | 75 g/h | Build tolerance; **lock the exact race products here** |
| Specific / peak | 90 g/h | Race rate, race products, race timing — including the dress rehearsal |
| Taper | unchanged | Confirm, change nothing |

Nothing new on race day. Not a flavour, not a brand.

## 2. Carbohydrate loading

ACSM guidance is **10–12 g/kg/day across the 36–48 h before the race**. Done properly this
raises muscle glycogen 50–100% above resting and is worth roughly 2–3% in endurance
performance.

At realistic body masses this is a genuinely difficult eating task, which is why it wants **one
rehearsal** — load into the block's biggest race-pace long run, where there's still time to
adjust. That also rehearses the GI response and the 1.5–2 kg of water weight stored glycogen
brings with it.

On plant-based diets it's harder: reaching the target while keeping fibre low enough to avoid
GI trouble means leaning on white rice, pasta, potato, juice and sports drink, and dropping
high-fibre staples for those two days specifically.

## 3. Caffeine

**3–6 mg/kg about 60 minutes pre-race**, worth
[2–7% in endurance performance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9824573/) — among
the best-evidenced legal ergogenic aids available.

Start at the low-to-middle end rather than the top. Above 3 mg/kg the side-effect rate climbs —
tachycardia, jitters, GI upset — and a marathon is long enough that GI trouble costs more than
the ergogenic effect returns. Trial the dose in a long run, never first on race day.

## 4. Iron, for athletes on plant-based diets

Worth stating explicitly because plans routinely note the dietary pattern in a context line and
then never act on it.

Plant-based endurance athletes are a **compound risk group** for iron deficiency: non-heme iron
absorbs far less efficiently than heme iron, and endurance running adds exercise-induced
hepcidin elevation, foot-strike haemolysis, and sweat and GI losses on top. **Iron deficiency
without anaemia measurably reduces endurance capacity, and correcting it restores that
capacity** — so a normal haemoglobin does not rule the problem out. Ferritin is the marker.

- Minimum panel: **serum ferritin + haemoglobin + transferrin saturation**.
- Common athlete guidance: symptoms can appear below ~30 ng/mL; keep ferritin above ~40 ng/mL
  for reserve. Cutoffs vary by source and lab — interpret with a clinician, not a blog table.
- Quarterly screening is the usual recommendation at high training volumes.

**Timing is the whole point.** Oral repletion of low ferritin takes roughly **three months**, so
a test is only actionable if it happens early enough in the block for a low result to be fixed.
Discovering it late — because the legs went flat — is information you can't use.

Do not supplement iron without testing first. Iron overload is a real harm and speculative
supplementation is not benign.

## 5. Protein

Endurance guidance is **1.4–1.8 g/kg/day**, and plant-based athletes are generally advised to
add ~10% for lower digestibility and leucine content. With two strength sessions a week, the
upper half of that range is the right place, spread across meals rather than backloaded, at
roughly 2 g leucine per meal. Blended plant sources (pea + rice) match whey for muscle protein
synthesis in trial data.

This matters more than it looks: strength work in a marathon plan exists to buy running economy
and late-race durability. Under-fuelled protein makes those sessions cost their recovery
without paying their adaptation.

## 6. Race-week checklist

- Carb load at 10–12 g/kg/day, low fibre, as rehearsed.
- Caffeine at the trialled dose, ~60 min pre-start.
- Race-morning meal: the one already rehearsed, at the same clock interval.
- In-race: locked products at the trained rate, at pre-decided time points.
- Shoes: race in shoes already run in. Advanced-plate footwear is worth a few percent in
  economy but wants kilometres on it, not a box opened the day before.
- Pacing: even-to-slightly-negative splits. The first 5 km feeling too easy is correct.

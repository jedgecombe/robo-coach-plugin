# Training-load rules: what the evidence actually supports

Shareable note — no athlete data. The private working version, with this block's
worked examples, lives outside `notes/shared/`.

Written because a generated training plan states trial evidence, coaching convention and
folklore-with-a-number in exactly the same confident voice. They are indistinguishable by
style. One of ours turned out to be folklore, and it had already changed real decisions before
anyone checked it.

## The 10% rule does not survive testing

The rule — *"don't increase weekly volume by more than ~10%"* — is one of the most repeated
claims in running, and it fails when tested directly.

- [Buist et al. 2008 (GRONORUN)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17940147/) randomised **532
  novice runners** to a 13-week graded programme built on the 10% rule, versus a standard
  8-week programme averaging **~24%** weekly increases. Injury incidence: **20.8% vs 20.3%**.
  No difference.
- [Systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/) of training-load
  change and running injury: no evidence supports the 10% rule as a progression guideline.
- Its more sophisticated successor, the **acute:chronic workload ratio**, has been
  [substantially discredited on statistical grounds](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01280-1)
  — mathematical coupling producing spurious correlation, arbitrary time windows, and
  instability whenever chronic load is low. Treat outliers properly and the relationship
  disappears.

Note what is *not* being claimed. Progressive overload is real, and ramping volume carelessly
is a bad idea. What fails is the specific threshold, and — more importantly — the practice of
**automating a decision from a percentage**.

## Two failure modes this creates in practice

**1. It fires on every planned down-week rebound.** A block that steps 64 → 82 km coming out
of a recovery week trips a 10% rule by design. The denominator is deliberately small. This is
the low-chronic-load instability the ACWR critiques identify, reproduced in a simpler metric,
and it trains you to ignore your own rule — which is worse than not having one.

**2. It converts arithmetic errors into accusations.** If planned volume is scored one way and
actual volume another, the ratio between them is meaningless, and a rule that fires on that
ratio will tell you a compliant athlete is overshooting. Ours did, across two weekly reviews,
until the athlete corrected the coach. **A rule that can manufacture a false accusation from a
spreadsheet mistake needs a much higher evidential bar than this one had.**

## What to use instead

Judge load on the athlete, not on a percentage. A large week-over-week jump is a **prompt to
check four things**, not a trigger:

1. **Symptoms** — niggles, morning stiffness, anything that changed gait. A rule that overrides
   a pain-free athlete's good week is worse than no rule.
2. **Objective drift at a fixed reference.** Establish an easy-pace heart-rate reference on
   recovered legs and watch it. HR climbing at that same pace, across more than one run, is
   real signal — and unlike a percentage it measures *this* athlete rather than last week's
   arithmetic.
3. **Sleep and life load.**
4. **How the quality sessions actually felt** — RPE, not just splits.

All four clean, the week stands, however large the jump. Cut for evidence of strain, never for
arithmetic alone.

The one thing still worth catching hard is **unplanned accumulation** — volume drifting upward
through sessions that were never in the plan. That's a different failure mode from executing a
planned rebound, and it's the one a load column genuinely helps you see. Keep the ramp figure
as an *observation*; stop treating it as a threshold.

## The general lesson: tag your rules

If a plan is going to carry rules, each one should say what it rests on:

- `[evidence]` — supported by trials or meta-analysis
- `[convention]` — standard practice, sound but not directly tested
- `[preference]` — the athlete's choice, not an empirical claim

The value isn't pedantry. It's that when a rule and reality disagree, you need to know
immediately whether you're overriding a finding or a habit. Rules that arrive pre-tagged get
re-examined; rules that arrive in a uniform confident voice do not.

Applying the tags is also a good audit in itself. Ours came out four `[evidence]`, three
`[convention]`, one `[preference]` and one mixed — and seeing which rules landed in
`[convention]` was more informative than the exercise had any right to be.
